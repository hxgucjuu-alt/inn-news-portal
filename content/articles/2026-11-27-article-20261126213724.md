  # Academic Research Trends in Public Space Governance and Spatial Planning for Immigrant Communities in North American Cities
  
  > This article explores the informal placemaking strategies of immigrant communities in North American cities, analyzes how urban planners and public administration departments address urban informality, and connects the current status of relevant academic research and educational resources.
  
  As North American cities face rapidly changing social structures, the definition and governance of public space have become core issues in the field of urban planning. According to academic research, "urban informality" has become an undeniable phenomenon in the development of cities in the Global North. Particularly in major U.S. cities, immigrant groups use "informal placemaking" strategies to carve out spaces within existing urban frameworks that serve cultural identity and social functions.
  
  This phenomenon not only challenges traditional urban design frameworks but also compels public administrators to re-examine existing land-use regulations and public space management policies. The research of urban planning scholar Ryan Thomas Devlin focuses on this, exploring how these informal behaviors interact and conflict with formal urban governance mechanisms, and further investigating how planners respond to these spontaneous spatial needs.
  
  Multiple academic and industry sources confirm that urban planning and public space governance in North America are in a period of transition. In relevant research from higher education institutions such as Temple University, there has been in-depth exploration of interactive technologies and planning theories for urban spaces. For example, through the application of computer science and virtual environment technology, researchers are able to simulate and analyze the interaction patterns between humans and machines in urban public spaces. This interdisciplinary research method provides new data support for understanding spatial usage behaviors in modern cities.
  
  At the same time, through the academic network of the Tyler School of Art and Architecture at Temple University, one can observe the academic focus on how immigrant groups establish a sense of belonging in cities through informal means, which highly overlaps with the challenges faced by urban public administration departments when formulating land-use policies.
  
  However, there are significant differences and room for interpretation regarding the definition of "public space" across different sources. BBC reports focus on the political and social emotional expression in public spaces in East Asian cities post-pandemic, emphasizing how public activities serve as an outlet for social emotions; this differs significantly in motivation and social context from the "informal placemaking by immigrant groups" focused on in North American urban planning research. In the North American context, the discussion focuses on urban design, immigrant integration, and the coordination of public administration; whereas in reports from other regions, public space is more often viewed as a site for collective memory and social movements.
  
  Furthermore, there is no unified policy consensus on how to balance the openness and security of public space, which remains a key issue to be clarified in current urban studies.
  
  Such issues have profound implications for industries and policies in North America. First, in terms of urban planning policy, local governments must strike a balance between maintaining urban order and accommodating the needs for diverse cultural spaces. If the creation of informal spaces is overly restricted, it may lead to the marginalization of immigrant groups in the city; conversely, a lack of an effective management framework may trigger disputes over public safety and land use. At the industry level, this also affects real estate development and the planning of public infrastructure; developers and urban designers need to be more sensitive to the informal interaction needs within communities and incorporate them into planning considerations to enhance the social resilience of public spaces.
  
  In addition, relevant educational institutions and research units must continue to provide data support to assist the government in formulating more flexible urban development strategies.
  
  Although research on urban informality by academia and administrative departments is deepening, there are limitations that should not be over-interpreted. First, the informal placemaking strategies of immigrant groups are not a single model; their forms of expression vary greatly depending on cultural background, economic conditions, and the regulatory environment of the city, and should not be simplified into a single social phenomenon. Second, the definition of "urban informality" in academic research often carries a specific theoretical perspective, and when applied to actual public policy formulation, the specificities of different communities must be considered.
  
  Furthermore, although technical means (such as virtual environment simulation) can assist in planning, they cannot completely replace the real interaction and negotiation process between people in physical public spaces; over-reliance on data models may overlook the emotional and cultural values in spatial usage.
  
  Subsequent observation should focus on how North American city governments translate these academic research findings into concrete policy tools. In particular, for areas with high concentrations of immigrant groups, whether local governments will establish more inclusive public space participation mechanisms will be an indicator for future urban governance. At the same time, as urban planning technology evolves, how to use digital tools to improve the efficiency of public space governance while ensuring that these tools do not become barriers that exclude specific groups is also a direction worth noting. The depth of dialogue between academia and public administration will directly affect the future development of public spaces in North American cities, and all sectors should continue to track the implementation effectiveness of relevant policies and community feedback.

# 北美城市公共空間治理與移民群體空間規劃之學術研究趨勢

> 本文探討北美城市中移民群體的非正式空間營造策略，分析都市規劃者與公共行政部門如何應對都市非正式性，並連結相關學術研究與教育資源的現況。


北美城市在面對快速變遷的社會結構時，公共空間的定義與治理成為都市規劃領域的核心議題。根據學術研究指出，都市非正式性（Urban Informality）已成為全球北方（Global North）城市發展中不可忽視的現象。特別是在美國各大城市，移民群體透過非正式的空間營造策略（Informal Placemaking），在既有的都市架構中開闢出具有文化認同與社交功能的場域。
這種現象不僅挑戰了傳統的都市設計框架，也迫使公共行政管理者必須重新審視現有的土地使用規範與公共空間管理政策。都市規劃學者 Ryan Thomas Devlin 的研究即聚焦於此，探討這些非正式行為如何與正式的城市治理機制產生互動與摩擦，並進一步探討規劃者如何回應這些自發性的空間需求。
多項學術與產業資訊共同確認，北美地區的都市規劃與公共空間治理正處於轉型期。在高等教育機構如天普大學（Temple University）的相關研究中，針對都市空間的互動技術與規劃理論已有深入探討。例如，透過電腦科學與虛擬環境技術的應用，研究者得以模擬並分析人類與機器在都市公共空間中的互動模式。這種跨領域的研究方法，為理解現代都市中的空間使用行為提供了新的資料支援。
同時，透過天普大學泰勒藝術與建築學院（Tyler School of Art and Architecture）的學術網路，可以觀察到學界對於移民群體在城市中如何透過非正式手段建立歸屬感的關注，這與城市公共行政部門在制定土地使用政策時所面臨的挑戰高度重疊。
然而，關於「公共空間」的定義在不同來源中存在顯著的差異與詮釋空間。BBC 的報導側重於東亞城市在疫情後公共空間的政治與社會情緒表達，強調公共活動如何成為社會情緒的釋放出口，這與北美城市規劃研究中關注的「移民群體非正式空間營造」在動機與社會背景上有顯著不同。在北美語境下，討論焦點多集中於都市設計、移民融合與公共行政的協調；而在其他地區的報導中，公共空間則更多被視為集體記憶與社會運動的場域。
此外，關於如何平衡公共空間的開放性與安全性，各界尚無統一的政策共識，這也是目前都市研究中待釐清的關鍵議題。
此類議題對於北美地區的產業與政策影響深遠。首先，在都市規劃政策方面，地方政府必須在維持城市秩序與包容多元文化空間需求之間取得平衡。若過度限制非正式空間的營造，可能導致移民群體在城市中的邊緣化；反之，若缺乏有效的管理框架，則可能引發公共安全與土地使用的爭議。在產業層面，這也影響了房地產開發與公共基礎設施的規劃，開發商與城市設計師需更敏銳地察覺社群內部的非正式互動需求，將其納入規劃考量，以提升公共空間的社會韌性。
此外，相關的教育機構與研究單位亦需持續提供資料支援，協助政府制定更具彈性的都市發展策略。
儘管學界與行政部門對於都市非正式性的研究日益深入，但仍存在不應過度解讀的限制。首先，移民群體的非正式空間營造策略並非單一模式，其表現形式會因文化背景、經濟條件及所在城市的法規環境而有巨大差異，不應將其簡化為單一的社會現象。其次，學術研究中對於「都市非正式性」的定義往往帶有特定的理論視角，在應用於實際的公共政策制定時，必須考量不同社群的特殊性。
此外，雖然技術手段（如虛擬環境模擬）能輔助規劃，但無法完全取代人與人之間在實體公共空間中的真實互動與協商過程，過度依賴資料模型可能忽略了空間使用中的情感與文化價值。
後續觀察重點應聚焦於北美城市政府如何將這些學術研究成果轉化為具體的政策工具。特別是針對移民群體高度集中的區域，地方政府是否會建立更具包容性的公共空間參與機制，將是未來都市治理的觀察指標。同時，隨著都市規劃技術的演進，如何利用數位工具提升公共空間的治理效率，並確保這些工具不會成為排斥特定群體的門檻，也是值得關注的方向。學術界與公共行政部門之間的對話深度，將直接影響未來北美城市公共空間的發展樣貌，各界應持續追蹤相關政策的執行成效與社群的回饋反應。
