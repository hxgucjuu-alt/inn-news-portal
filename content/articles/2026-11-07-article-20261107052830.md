  # AI Data Center Expansion Triggers Water Crisis: One-Quarter of Global Facilities Face Water Scarcity Risks
  
  > Recent research indicates that approximately 25% of data centers worldwide are located in regions with water supply and demand imbalances. With the rapid development of AI technology, the massive demand for cooling water is posing a severe challenge to local water resource management, and the risk of water shortages is expected to climb further in the future.
  
  The explosive growth in demand for artificial intelligence (AI) computing has become a core driving force for the global technology industry; however, the environmental costs hidden behind this growth are gradually emerging. According to recent research published in the journal *Environmental Science & Technology* by academic institutions including the Carnegie Institution for Science, the contradiction between the geographical distribution of global data centers and regional water resource supplies is becoming increasingly acute.
  
  The study conducted a detailed location analysis of 9,347 data centers worldwide, incorporating data on local rainfall, current water supply status, and demands for agricultural and domestic water use, aiming to assess the survival and operational risks of this energy-intensive infrastructure against the backdrop of climate change. As global warming intensifies, the scarcity of water resources has evolved from a regional issue into a common test for global technological infrastructure. In particular, in Oceania and other island nations that rely on stable water supplies, data center expansion strategies are facing pressure for reassessment.
  
  Multiple sources have confirmed that approximately 25% of data centers worldwide are currently located in regions that face a water supply-demand gap for at least one month per year. This data reveals that the technology industry, in its pursuit of high-performance computing, often overlooks the infrastructure's reliance on environmental resources. The study points out that to maintain the efficient operation of servers, data centers must consume large amounts of cooling water, which undoubtedly exacerbates competition for water with local residents, agricultural irrigation, and other critical industries in regions where water resources are already strained.
  
  As the demand for AI model training and inference continues to climb, the cooling load and power consumption of data centers increase in tandem, leading to non-linear growth in water consumption. This has become a public issue in global climate and energy policy that cannot be ignored.
  
  Regarding the assessment of future risks, there are some discrepancies between different sources and research models. While all parties agree that AI expansion will exacerbate water pressure, specific forecast data on risk escalation varies. The research team estimated, under simulated climate warming scenarios, that the proportion of data centers threatened by water scarcity risks could rise from the current 25% to 33%. However, this projection remains subject to variables, as it depends on the speed of adjustments to regional water management policies, the evolution of data center cooling technologies, and changes in rainfall patterns in future climate models.
  
  What remains unconfirmed is whether governments will formulate stricter water quotas for data centers or mandate that companies adopt closed-loop cooling systems to reduce reliance on external water sources; these policy details remain a focus of ongoing negotiations between the technology industry and local governments.
  
  Regarding the specific impact on Oceania and other parts of the world, the issue of water consumption by data centers has evolved from a technical challenge into a public issue with social and economic dimensions. The Oceania region is composed of numerous islands, where water is a precious and fragile natural resource; if data centers expand in this region, it will directly impact the limited local freshwater supply. Furthermore, if the expansion of the industrial chain does not consider the sustainability of water resources, it may lead to risks of operational disruption for companies and even trigger disputes over water rights with local communities.
  
  Policymakers must strike a balance between promoting digital transformation and protecting the environment, ensuring that the development of the AI industry does not come at the expense of local public water safety.
  
  Although the research data clearly warns of water scarcity risks, there are still limitations to the perspectives held by various parties. Some industry analysts argue that the risks in a single region should not be over-interpreted, as many advanced data centers have begun to incorporate climate-adaptive cooling technologies or reduce reliance on public water supply systems through water recycling systems. Additionally, the research results are primarily based on statistical analysis and do not fully account for individual mitigation measures that companies may take in specific regions. Therefore, it is inaccurate to view the 25% of global data centers as all facing an immediate threat of water shortage; the data more reflects a systemic blind spot in global data center siting strategies regarding water management, rather than an operational judgment on any single facility.
  
  Subsequent observations will focus on how technology companies adjust their siting strategies and whether governments will include a "water footprint" as a necessary condition for data center access reviews. As the effects of climate change intensify, it is expected that more research will be conducted to evaluate the cooling water efficiency of data centers and promote the formulation of relevant standards. For water-sensitive regions like Oceania, how to combine local climate conditions to develop low-water-consumption digital infrastructure will become a key indicator for measuring the sustainability of the region's digitalization process. Relevant units need to continuously monitor data center water usage data and ensure that an effective dynamic balance can be achieved between technological development and environmental carrying capacity.

# AI資料中心擴張引發水資源危機：全球四分之一設施面臨缺水風險

> 最新研究顯示，全球約25%的資料中心位於水資源供需失衡地區，隨AI技術快速發展，龐大的冷卻用水需求正對地方水資源管理構成嚴峻挑戰，未來缺水風險恐進一步攀升。


人工智慧（AI）運算需求的爆發式成長，已成為全球科技產業的核心驅動力，然而其背後隱藏的環境成本正逐漸浮現。根據美國卡內基科學研究所（Carnegie Institution for Science）等學術機構近期發表於《環境科學與技術》（Environmental Science & Technology）期刊的最新研究，全球資料中心的地理分佈與區域水資源供應之間的矛盾日益尖銳。
該研究針對全球共計9,347座資料中心進行了詳細的位置分析，並結合各地降雨量、水資源供應現況及農業、民生用水需求資料，旨在評估這些高耗能基礎設施在氣候變遷背景下的生存與營運風險。隨著全球氣候暖化趨勢加劇，水資源的稀缺性已從區域性問題演變為全球科技基礎設施的共同考驗，特別是在大洋洲及其他依賴穩定水資源供應的島嶼國家，資料中心的擴張策略正面臨重新評估的壓力。
多項來源共同確認，目前全球約有25%的資料中心座落於每年至少有一個月面臨水資源供需缺口的地區。這項資料揭示了科技產業在追求高效能運算時，往往忽略了基礎設施對環境資源的依賴性。研究指出，為了維持伺服器的高效運轉，資料中心必須消耗大量的冷卻用水，這在水資源原本就緊張的地區，無疑加劇了與當地居民、農業灌溉及其他關鍵工業之間的用水競爭。
隨著AI模型訓練與推論需求的持續攀升，資料中心的冷卻負荷與電力消耗同步增加，進而導致水資源消耗量呈現非線性成長，這已成為全球氣候與能源政策中不可忽視的公共議題。
針對未來風險的評估，不同來源與研究模型之間存在部分差異。雖然各界均認同AI擴張將加劇用水壓力，但對於風險升級的具體預測資料則有所不同。研究團隊在模擬氣候暖化情境下推估，受缺水風險威脅的資料中心比例可能從目前的25%上升至33%。然而，這項推估資料仍存在變數，因為其取決於各地區水資源管理政策的調整速度、資料中心冷卻技術的演進，以及未來氣候模型中降雨模式的變化。
目前尚未確認的是，各國政府是否會針對資料中心制定更為嚴格的用水配額，或是強制要求企業採用封閉式冷卻系統以降低對外部水源的依賴，這些政策細節仍是科技產業與地方政府之間持續協商的焦點。
對於大洋洲及全球各地的具體影響而言，資料中心的水資源消耗問題已不僅是技術層面的挑戰，更演變為社會與經濟層面的公共議題。大洋洲地區由眾多島嶼組成，水資源本就屬於珍貴且脆弱的自然資源，若資料中心在該區域擴張，將直接衝擊當地有限的淡水供應。此外，產業鏈的擴張若未考慮水資源的可持續性，可能導致企業在經營過程中面臨營運中斷的風險，甚至引發與在地社群的用水權益糾紛。
政策制定者必須在推動數位轉型與維護環境生態之間取得平衡，確保AI產業的發展不會以犧牲地方公共用水安全為代價。
儘管研究資料明確警示了缺水風險，但各界觀點仍存在限制。部分產業分析師認為，不應過度解讀單一地區的風險，因為許多先進的資料中心已開始匯入氣候適應型冷卻技術，或是透過水資源回收系統減少對公共供水系統的依賴。此外，研究結果主要基於統計分析，並未完全考慮企業在特定地區可能採取的個別緩解措施。因此，將全球25%的資料視為所有資料中心皆面臨立即性缺水威脅並不準確，該資料更多是反映了全球資料中心選址策略在水資源管理上的系統性盲點，而非針對單一設施的經營判斷。
後續觀察重點將聚焦於科技企業如何調整其選址策略，以及政府是否會將「水資源足跡」納入資料中心准入審查的必要條件。隨著氣候變遷影響加劇，預期未來將有更多研究針對資料中心的冷卻用水效率進行評估，並推動相關標準的制定。對於大洋洲等水資源敏感地區，如何結合在地氣候條件發展低耗水型的數位基礎設施，將成為衡量該區域數位化程式是否具備永續性的關鍵指標。相關單位需持續監測資料中心用水資料，並確保科技發展與環境承載力之間能達成有效的動態平衡。
