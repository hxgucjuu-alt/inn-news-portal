  # Resilient Governance and Cross-Border Cooperation in the Asia-Pacific: The Dual Challenges of Climate Change and Tourism Impacts
  
  > As global climate change and demographic shifts intensify, the Asia-Pacific region is seeking governance solutions through the "Global Resilience Symposium" and "Mekong Sub-regional Cooperation," while simultaneously facing social order challenges stemming from over-tourism.
  
  In September 2026, the University of Taipei and the Civil Service Development Institute of the Directorate-General of Personnel Administration, Executive Yuan, jointly hosted the "2026 Global Resilience International Symposium." The event brought together scholars from nine countries—Taiwan, Japan, Italy, New Zealand, Bulgaria, Malaysia, Thailand, South Korea, and the United Kingdom—to engage in in-depth dialogues on issues such as climate change, AI development, and urban planning. Concurrently, cross-border cooperation in Southeast Asia continues to deepen, particularly regarding economic integration and sustainable development in the Mekong sub-region.
  
  According to public records, the 9th Lancang-Mekong Cooperation (LMC) Foreign Ministers' Meeting was held in Chiang Mai, Thailand, in August 2024. Subsequently, the "Declaration of the 8th Greater Mekong Subregion (GMS) Summit," released in November of the same year, explicitly emphasized the need for member states to address future regional economic transformation challenges through cultural innovation and resource sharing.
  
  Multiple sources consistently point out that the core of current governance in the Asia-Pacific region lies in the establishment of "resilience." Chiu Ying-hao, President of the University of Taipei, emphasized at the symposium that universities should act as think tanks for urban change, providing concrete solutions for climate change and demographic shifts. At the regional cooperation level, GMS member states have reached a consensus to achieve sustainable sub-regional growth by deepening cross-border connectivity, coordinating economic policies, and pooling technical resources. Singapore, as a benchmark for climate governance in the region, is viewed as a model for other countries, demonstrating that Southeast Asian nations are actively shifting from localized governance to regional collaboration in the face of climate change impacts.
  
  However, there are discrepancies among information sources regarding the definition and implementation paths of resilient governance. The symposium focused on academic and policy dialogues concerning higher education and urban planning, emphasizing the enhancement of urban shock-absorption capacity through the optimization of public services. In contrast, the declarations related to the LMC focus on economic integration and infrastructure connectivity, emphasizing the release of economic potential through a "community with a shared future" framework.
  
  Furthermore, regarding the social impact of tourism—such as the recent "excretion terrorism" controversy in Kabukicho and Kamakura, Japan—reports indicate that these issues are no longer isolated to a single country but are common governance challenges faced by popular tourist destinations globally. However, current academic seminars and regional economic cooperation mechanisms have yet to propose a concrete transnational framework to address the breakdown of social order caused by over-tourism.
  
  These governance challenges have a direct impact on industries and policies within the region. Economically, cross-border economic connectivity in the Mekong sub-region is expected to drive the transformation of logistics, digital technology, and infrastructure industries, but it also requires higher levels of policy coordination among nations to ensure environmental and social sustainability during the economic transition. Socially, the management of the environment and order in tourism hotspots has become a heavy burden for local governments, forcing countries to rethink the meaning of "resilience"—not just as the ability to cope with natural disasters, but as social resilience in the face of human activity. Failure to effectively address the negative externalities of tourism may weaken the competitiveness of cities in the region to attract international talent and investment.
  
  Regarding differing perspectives and limitations, it is important to note the interpretation of the term "resilience" in various contexts. Academia tends to view resilience as a systemic adaptive capacity, emphasizing long-term planning in education and policy; policymakers, however, are more inclined to translate resilience into concrete economic growth indicators and cross-border cooperation performance. Additionally, some sources are overly optimistic about climate change responses, overlooking the funding gaps and technical barriers faced by developing countries during industrial transformation. Therefore, regional cooperation declarations should not be viewed as a panacea for all problems; implementation remains constrained by differences in political systems and uneven progress in infrastructure construction, and over-interpreting the immediate effects of regional cooperation may lead to biased conclusions.
  
  Future observation should focus on: first, whether the "urban resilience" recommendations proposed by the nine scholars at the Taipei symposium can be effectively translated into concrete policies by local governments; second, how the Mekong sub-region will coordinate conflicts of interest between environmental protection and economic development while implementing the 8th Summit Declaration; and third, whether transnational norms or city alliances similar to "resilient governance" will emerge internationally to address the pressure on urban infrastructure caused by out-of-control tourist behavior. These issues will continue to test the Asia-Pacific region's comprehensive ability to integrate academic research, policy formulation, and social management in the face of globalization shocks.

# 亞太地區韌性治理與跨境合作：氣候變遷與觀光衝擊的雙重挑戰

> 隨全球氣候變遷與人口轉型加劇，亞太地區正透過「全球韌性研討會」與「湄公河次區域合作」尋求治理對策，同時面臨觀光過度發展衍生的社會秩序挑戰。


2026年9月，臺北市立大學與行政院人事行政總處公務人力發展學院共同舉辦「2026全球韌性國際研討會」，邀集臺灣、日本、義大利、紐西蘭、保加利亞、馬來西亞、泰國、韓國及英國等九國學者，針對氣候變遷、AI發展及都市規劃等議題進行深度對話。與此同時，東南亞地區的跨境合作亦在持續深化，特別是針對湄公河次區域的經濟整合與永續發展。
根據公開資料顯示，瀾滄江—湄公河合作機制（瀾湄合作）第九次外長會於2024年8月在泰國清邁舉行，隨後於同年11月發布的「大湄公河次區域經濟合作第八次領導人會議宣言」，更明確強調成員國需透過創新文化與資源共享，應對未來的區域經濟轉型挑戰。
多個來源一致指出，當前亞太地區的治理核心在於「韌性」的建立。臺北市立大學校長邱英浩於研討會中強調，大學應扮演城市變局的智庫角色，為氣候變遷與人口轉型提供具體解方。在區域合作層面，大湄公河次區域的成員國已達成共識，將透過深化跨境通道、協調經濟政策及匯集技術資源，以實現次區域的永續增長。新加坡作為區域內氣候治理的指標，其應對氣候危機的模式亦被視為其他國家參考的典範，顯示出東南亞各國在面對氣候變遷衝擊時，正積極從單點治理轉向區域協作。
然而，資訊來源間對於韌性治理的定義與實踐路徑存在差異。研討會側重於高等教育與都市規劃的學術與政策對話，強調透過公共服務的最佳化來提升城市承受衝擊的能力；而瀾湄合作的相關宣言則聚焦於經濟一體化與基礎設施聯通，強調透過「命運共同體」的架構來釋放經濟潛力。
此外，針對觀光帶來的社會衝擊，如日本歌舞伎町與鎌倉近期面臨的「排洩恐怖主義」爭議，報導指出此類問題已非單一國家現象，而是全球熱門觀光地區共同面臨的治理難題，但目前相關學術研討與區域經濟合作機制中，對於觀光過度導致的社會秩序崩壞，尚未提出具體的跨國解決框架。
這些治理挑戰對區域內的產業與政策產生直接影響。在經濟面，湄公河次區域的跨境經濟聯通，預計將帶動物流、數位科技與基礎建設產業的轉型，但也要求各國在政策上進行更高度的協調，以確保經濟轉型過程中的環境與社會永續。在社會面，觀光熱點的環境與秩序管理已成為地方政府的沉重負擔，這迫使各國必須重新思考「韌性」的內涵——不僅是應對天災的韌性，更包含應對人為活動衝擊的社會韌性。若無法有效解決觀光帶來的負面外部效應，將可能削弱區域內城市吸引國際人才與投資的競爭力。
不同觀點與限制方面，需注意「韌性」一詞在不同語境下的解讀。學術界傾向於將韌性視為一種系統性的調適能力，強調教育與政策的長期佈局；而政策制定者則更傾向於將韌性轉化為具體的經濟成長指標與跨境合作績效。此外，部分來源對於氣候變遷的應對過於樂觀，忽略了開發中國家在產業轉型過程中，面臨的資金缺口與技術門檻。因此，不應將區域合作宣言視為解決所有問題的萬靈丹，各國在執行層面仍受限於政治體制差異與基礎設施建設進度的不均，過度解讀區域合作的即時成效恐失之偏頗。
後續觀察重點應聚焦於：第一，九國學者在臺北研討會提出的「城市韌性」建議，是否能有效轉化為地方政府的具體政策；第二，湄公河次區域在落實第八次領導人會議宣言時，如何協調各國在環境保護與經濟開發之間的利益衝突；第三，針對全球性觀光秩序問題，國際間是否會出現類似「韌性治理」的跨國規範或城市聯盟，以共同應對遊客行為失控對城市基礎設施造成的壓力。這些議題將持續考驗亞太地區在面對全球化衝擊時，整合學術研究、政策制定與社會管理的綜合實力。
