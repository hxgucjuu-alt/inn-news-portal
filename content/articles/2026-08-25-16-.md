  # New Zealand Proposes Law to Ban Social Media for Under-16s, with Heavy Fines for Violating Platforms
  
  > The New Zealand government has formally submitted the Online Safety Bill to Parliament, proposing to ban social media use for those under 16, with non-compliant platforms facing fines of up to 10% of global turnover. However, whether the bill can pass before Parliament dissolves in October remains uncertain.
  
  The New Zealand government this week formally advanced a high-profile youth online protection policy, planning to legislate a comprehensive ban on the use of social media platforms by children and teenagers under 16. According to multiple international media reports, the draft legislation, named the Online Safety Bill, has been submitted to the New Zealand Parliament. If passed, it would make New Zealand one of the few countries in the world to explicitly prohibit a specific age group from using social media by law.
  
  Prime Minister Christopher Luxon stated publicly that the move is in response to "the harm being done to a generation," highlighting the government's strong emphasis on youth mental health and online safety issues.
  
  According to combined international reports, the core content of the bill requires social media platforms, including Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, and Facebook, to take effective measures to verify users' ages, ensuring users are at least 16 years old. Platforms that fail to comply would face fines of up to 10% of their global turnover. This penalty design clearly draws on the regulatory approach of the European Union's Digital Services Act, aiming to pressure major tech companies into assuming greater social responsibility through economic sanctions.
  
  When announcing the policy, Prime Minister Luxon cited data indicating that about one-third of New Zealand teenagers aged 13 to 17 spend at least five hours per day on social media, a statistic that serves as an important basis for the government's push for legislation.
  
  However, across multiple media reports, there are notable discrepancies regarding the bill's legislative process and prospects for passage. Reports from the UAE's Al Etihad and China's China.org.cn both frame the story as "New Zealand government advances the ban," emphasizing that the bill has been formally submitted to Parliament and presenting a positive momentum for policy advancement. In contrast, a report from U.S.-based ABC News is more cautious, explicitly stating that the bill currently "lacks enough support to pass before Parliament dissolves in October," suggesting the legislative timeline may be hindered by political factors.
  
  This difference in reporting angles reflects divergent judgments among media outlets regarding the certainty and optimism of the same policy event, and also indicates that the bill's ultimate fate remains highly uncertain.
  
  If this legislation is implemented, it would have profound impacts on New Zealand society. At the educational level, schools and parents may face significant adjustments in how they supervise teenagers' social media use, shifting from a model that relied on family self-management to one mandated by national law. At the industry level, major tech companies would be forced to invest substantial resources in developing and deploying age verification technologies, including biometric identification and identity document review systems. This not only increases operating costs but may also trigger a new round of controversy over privacy protections.
  
  Additionally, this policy could affect how New Zealand teenagers access information and build social connections. Particularly for teenagers in remote areas or from disadvantaged families, social media is often an important channel for connecting with the outside world, and a blanket ban could exacerbate the digital divide.
  
  There are diverse views and concerns across society regarding this policy. Supporters argue that, given the substantial research evidence on the negative effects of social media on teenagers' mental health, the government has a responsibility to protect minors through mandatory measures, and that hefty fines can effectively deter platforms from passive responses. Opponents worry that age verification mechanisms may infringe on user privacy, and that simply banning use is not a fundamental solution, overlooking the importance of digital literacy education. Some experts also question whether teenagers might circumvent restrictions by falsifying their age or using virtual private networks (VPNs), significantly undermining the bill's practical effectiveness.
  
  Notably, none of the current reports mention the explicit stance of New Zealand's main opposition parties on this bill, nor do they detail the specific views of Māori community or other minority group representatives on the policy—both are aspects that cannot be ignored when assessing the bill's social acceptance.
  
  Furthermore, many details of the bill remain to be clarified. First, the scope of the definition of "social media platforms" under the bill—whether it covers instant messaging apps, in-game chat functions, or emerging AI social applications—has not been clearly specified. Second, the specific technical standards and implementation procedures for age verification have not been announced; whether platforms will adopt a particular verification method, whether it will be uniformly regulated by the government or left to platforms' discretion, remains unknown. Third, whether the bill includes exemption clauses, such as parental consent mechanisms or exemptions for educational purposes, has not been mentioned in the reports. Finally, if the bill fails to pass before October, whether the next Parliament will reconsider it and whether its content will be adjusted are key points for future observation.
  
  The international community will also closely follow New Zealand's legislative experience as a reference case for other countries developing similar policies.

# 紐西蘭擬立法禁16歲以下使用社群媒體 違規業者恐遭重罰

> 紐西蘭政府正式向國會提交《網路安全法案》，計劃禁止16歲以下青少年使用社群媒體，違規平臺最高可處全球營業額10%罰款，但法案能否於十月國會解散前透過仍存變數。


紐西蘭政府本週正式推動一項備受矚目的青少年網路保護政策，計劃透過立法全面禁止16歲以下兒童及青少年使用社群媒體平臺。根據多家國際媒體報導，這項名為《網路安全法案》（Online Safety Bill）的立法草案已提交至紐西蘭國會，若順利透過，將使紐西蘭成為全球少數以法律明文禁止特定年齡層使用社群媒體的國家之一。
總理克里斯多福·盧克森（Christopher Luxon）在公開場合表示，此舉是為了回應「一個世代正在受到的傷害」，凸顯出該國政府對青少年心理健康與網路安全問題的高度重視。
綜合外電報導，這項法案的核心內容在於要求包括Instagram、TikTok、Snapchat及Facebook在內的社群媒體平臺，必須採取有效措施驗證使用者年齡，確保使用者年滿16歲。若平臺未能遵守規定，將面臨最高達其全球營業額10%的巨額罰款。此一罰則設計明顯參考了歐盟《數位服務法》的監管思路，意在透過經濟制裁壓力迫使大型科技公司承擔更多社會責任。
盧克森總理在宣佈此政策時引用資料指出，約有三分之一的13至17歲紐西蘭青少年每天花費至少五個小時在社群媒體上，這項資料成為政府推動立法的重要依據。
然而，在多家媒體的交叉報導中，關於法案的立法程式與透過前景存在明顯差異。阿聯酋《Al Etihad》與中國《中國網》的報導均以「紐西蘭政府推進禁令」為主要框架，強調法案已正式提交國會，呈現出政策推動的積極態勢。但美國《ABC News》的報導則更為審慎，明確指出該法案目前「缺乏足夠支援以在十月國會解散前透過」，暗示立法時程可能因政治因素而受阻。
這種報導角度的差異，反映出不同媒體對同一政策事件在確定性與樂觀程度上的判斷分歧，也顯示法案的最終命運仍存在高度不確定性。
這項立法若付諸實行，將對紐西蘭社會產生深遠影響。在教育層面，學校與家長對青少年使用社群媒體的監管方式可能面臨重大調整，過去依賴家庭自主管理的模式將轉變為國家法律強制規範。在產業層面，大型科技公司將被迫投入大量資源開發與部署年齡驗證技術，包括生物辨識、身分檔案審查等系統，這不僅增加營運成本，也可能引發關於隱私權保障的新一輪爭議。
此外，這項政策也可能影響紐西蘭青少年獲取資訊、建立社交連結的方式，特別是對於偏遠地區或弱勢家庭的青少年而言，社群媒體往往是他們與外界聯絡的重要管道，全面禁止可能加劇數位落差。
針對這項政策，社會各界存在不同觀點與疑慮。支持者認為，鑑於社群媒體對青少年心理健康造成的負面影響已有大量研究佐證，政府有責任透過強制手段保護未成年人，且高額罰款能有效嚇阻平臺業者消極應對。反對者則擔憂，年齡驗證機制可能侵犯使用者隱私，且單純禁止使用並非根本解決之道，忽視了數位素養教育的重要性。部分專家也質疑，青少年可能透過虛報年齡或使用虛擬私人網路（VPN）等方式規避限制，使法案實際執行效果大打折扣。
值得注意的是，目前所有報導均未提及紐西蘭主要反對黨對此法案的明確立場，也未說明毛利社群或其他少數族群代表對該政策的具體看法，這些都是評估法案社會接受度時不可忽略的面向。
此外，關於法案的諸多細節仍有待釐清。首先，法案對「社群媒體平臺」的定義範圍為何，是否涵蓋即時通訊軟體、遊戲內建聊天功能或新興的AI社交應用，目前尚無明確說明。其次，年齡驗證的具體技術標準與執行程式也未公佈，平臺業者將採用何種驗證方式、由政府統一規範或由業者自行選擇，均屬未知。再者，法案是否設有例外條款，例如家長同意機制或教育用途的豁免，報導中亦未提及。最後，若法案未能於十月前透過，未來新一屆國會是否會重新審議、內容是否調整，都將是後續觀察的重點。
國際社會也將密切關注紐西蘭的立法經驗，作為其他國家制定類似政策的參考案例。
