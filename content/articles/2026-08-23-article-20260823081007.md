  # Europe's AI Strategic Shift: A Critical Moment from Regulatory Leadership to Industrial Breakthrough
  
  > The European Union has recently proposed two AI strategies aimed at strengthening industrial and scientific competitiveness, while the Artificial Intelligence Act has been finalized. However, Europe remains highly dependent on American companies in the field of foundation models, and the gap between policy and industrial reality has become a focus of attention across sectors.
  
  Europe's artificial intelligence development has long ranked second globally, trailing only the United States, with the UK possessing the most powerful AI ecosystem and the highest number of startups in Europe, followed closely by Germany and France. According to an industry analysis by cieropencall.org, although the number of AI startups in major EU countries still lags behind the US, Europe's average level of development can be considered sound based on government AI development and readiness assessments from 2019. This evaluation indicates that Europe's foundational strength in AI is not weak, but rather exhibits a structural characteristic of "strong research capabilities with slow industrial transformation."
  
  Meanwhile, since 2018, the EU has successively launched national-level AI strategies, and member states have also established their own AI development blueprints, attempting to secure a place in the rapidly changing global AI race.
  
  The European Commission recently proposed two strategies focusing respectively on AI applications in industry and science, with the goal of keeping Europe at the forefront of AI. According to a report by zh-cn.eureporter.co, these two strategies outline how to accelerate the application of AI in business operations, public services, and scientific research, emphasizing that AI is expected to improve the lives of Europeans in many ways. Meanwhile, the legislative process for the EU's Artificial Intelligence Act reached a critical milestone on January 19, 2024, when the European Commission, the European Parliament, and the Council of the European Union jointly finalized the text of the regulation.
  
  According to a Chinese translation published by aisg.tongji.edu.cn, this regulation aims to establish harmonized rules on AI and amend certain Union legislative acts, with far-reaching implications for the global AI governance landscape.
  
  It is worth noting that different sources show significant discrepancies in their assessments of Europe's AI development. Data from cieropencall.org and Airiti Library tend to present Europe's overall AI competitiveness and policy readiness, emphasizing its "sound average level of development"; however, a report cited by hub.baai.ac.cn from the European Economic and Social Committee (EESC) and the French Institute of International Relations (IFRI) points out that Europe is heavily dependent on American companies in the field of foundation models, such as OpenAI's GPT-4 and Google's Gemini, while also underestimating China's momentum in AI development.
  
  The tension between these two narratives reflects the gap between "ideals and reality" in European AI policy—on the one hand, the EU attempts to establish a global regulatory benchmark through the Artificial Intelligence Act; on the other hand, European companies still struggle to break free from dependence on American tech giants in the underlying technologies of generative AI.
  
  This gap between policy and industrial reality has concrete impacts on relevant regions and industries in Europe. First, at the industrial level, although Europe has a considerable number of AI startups, it still lags behind the US and China in foundation model R&D and large-scale computing power deployment, which means European companies often need to pay American cloud service providers or model suppliers for AI applications, creating an unequal relationship in the value chain. Second, at the policy level, although the finalized Artificial Intelligence Act provides a clear regulatory framework for AI applications, the obligations imposed on foundation model developers and deployers may further raise compliance costs, placing an additional burden on startups with limited resources.
  
  Furthermore, although the two strategies proposed by the European Commission demonstrate ambition to strengthen industrial competitiveness, the specific resource allocation and implementation details remain to be seen.
  
  Regarding assessments of Europe's AI development, the tension between different perspectives deserves attention. On the one hand, data from cieropencall.org and Airiti Library show that Europe has a solid foundation in government AI readiness and research investment, particularly in major economies such as the UK, Germany, and France, where AI ecosystems have reached a considerable level of maturity; on the other hand, European think tank reports cited by hub.baai.ac.cn warn that Europe's dependence in the foundation model field may weaken its strategic autonomy, and underestimating China's AI development could also lead Europe to misjudge the situation in the global AI race.
  
  The coexistence of these different perspectives reminds us that when interpreting Europe's AI development, we should not oversimplify it into a single narrative. It should be noted that some sources do not indicate clear publication dates, and the baseline years of data across sources vary (such as the 2019 government readiness assessment versus the 2024 finalized regulation), so caution should be exercised when comparing differences in time spans.
  
  Key points for subsequent observation should focus on the following aspects: first, the implementation of the Artificial Intelligence Act after it takes effect, particularly the preparedness of regulatory bodies in member states and corporate compliance costs; second, whether the specific budget allocations and resource distribution for the European Commission's two strategies are sufficient to support Europe's independent R&D in the foundation model field; third, the coordination mechanism between the UK and the EU on AI policy after Brexit, as the UK possesses Europe's most powerful AI ecosystem and regulatory divergence with the EU may affect the development trajectory of Europe's overall AI industry; fourth, whether Europe's attitude toward China's AI development will shift in response to warnings from think tank reports, thereby affecting the balance of power in global AI governance.
  
  These issues will continue to shape Europe's position in the global AI landscape in the coming years.

# 歐洲AI戰略轉向：從監管領跑到產業突圍的關鍵時刻

> 歐盟近期提出兩項AI戰略，旨在強化產業與科學競爭力，同時《人工智慧法》定稿落地。然而，歐洲在基礎模型領域高度依賴美國企業，政策與產業現實之間的落差成為各界關注焦點。

歐洲人工智慧發展長期位居全球第二，僅次於美國，其中英國擁有最強大的AI生態系統，新創數量居歐洲之冠，德國與法國緊隨其後。根據cieropencall.org的產業評析，歐盟主要國家的AI新創數量雖與美國仍有一段落差，但從2019年政府AI發展與準備程度來看，歐洲平均發展水準堪稱良好。這項評估顯示，歐洲在AI領域的基礎實力並非薄弱，而是呈現「科研強、產業轉化慢」的結構性特徵。
與此同時，歐盟自2018年起陸續推出國家層級AI策略，各成員國也相繼訂立AI發展藍圖，試圖在快速變遷的全球AI競賽中佔有一席之地。
歐盟委員會近期提出兩項戰略，分別聚焦AI在產業與科學領域的應用，目標是讓歐洲在AI前沿保持領先地位。根據zh-cn.eureporter.co的報導，這兩項戰略闡述瞭如何加快AI在企業營運、公共服務與科學研究中的應用，並強調AI有望在諸多層面改善歐洲人的生活。與此同時，歐盟《人工智慧法》的立法程式也在2024年1月19日迎來關鍵節點，歐盟委員會、歐洲議會和歐盟理事會共同完成了該法規的定稿。
根據aisg.tongji.edu.cn發布的中文譯本，這部法規旨在制定AI的統一規則，並修訂若干聯盟立法，對全球AI治理格局具有深遠影響。
值得注意的是，不同來源對歐洲AI發展的評估存在明顯差異。cieropencall.org與Airiti Library的資料傾向呈現歐洲AI的整體競爭力與政策準備度，強調其「平均發展水準良好」的一面；然而，hub.baai.ac.cn引述歐洲經濟和社會委員會（EESC）與法國國際關係研究所（IFRI）的報告則指出，歐洲在基礎模型領域嚴重依賴美國企業，如OpenAI的GPT-4和Google的Gemini，同時低估了中國在AI領域的發展動能。
這兩種敘事之間的張力，反映出歐洲AI政策「理想與現實」之間的落差——一方面，歐盟試圖透過《人工智慧法》建立全球監管標竿；另一方面，歐洲企業在生成式AI的底層技術競爭中，仍難以擺脫對美國科技巨頭的依賴。
這項政策與產業現實的落差，對歐洲相關地區與產業帶來具體影響。首先，在產業層面，歐洲AI新創雖然數量可觀，但在基礎模型研發與大規模算力部署上，仍落後於美國與中國，這使得歐洲企業在AI應用層面往往需要向美國雲端服務商或模型供應商付費，形成價值鏈上的不對等關係。其次，在政策層面，《人工智慧法》的定稿雖然為AI應用提供了明確的監管框架，但對於基礎模型開發者與部署者的義務規範，可能進一步拉高合規成本，對資源有限的新創企業形成額外負擔。
此外，歐盟委員會提出的兩項戰略，雖然展現了強化產業競爭力的企圖心，但具體資源配置與執行細節仍待觀察。
對於歐洲AI發展的評估，不同觀點之間的張力值得留意。一方面，cieropencall.org與Airiti Library的資料顯示，歐洲在政府AI準備度與科研投入上具備良好基礎，特別是在英國、德國與法國等主要經濟體，AI生態系統已相當成熟；另一方面，hub.baai.ac.cn引述的歐洲智庫報告則警告，歐洲在基礎模型領域的依賴性可能削弱其戰略自主性，而對中國AI發展的低估，也可能導致歐洲在全球AI競賽中誤判局勢。
這些不同觀點的並存，提醒我們在解讀歐洲AI發展時，不宜過度簡化為單一敘事。需要注意的是，部分來源未標示明確發布時間，且各來源的資料基準年份不一（如2019年的政府準備度評估與2024年的法規定稿），在比較時應謹慎對待時間跨度的差異。
後續觀察重點應聚焦於以下幾個面向：第一，《人工智慧法》正式生效後的執行情況，特別是各成員國監管機構的整備程度與企業合規成本；第二，歐盟委員會兩項戰略的具體預算編列與資源分配，是否足以支撐歐洲在基礎模型領域的自主研發；第三，英國脫歐後與歐盟在AI政策上的協調機制，由於英國擁有歐洲最強大的AI生態系統，其與歐盟的監管分歧可能影響整體歐洲AI產業的發展路徑；第四，歐洲對中國AI發展的態度是否會因智庫報告的警示而調整，進而影響全球AI治理的權力平衡。
這些議題將在未來數年內，持續形塑歐洲在全球AI版圖中的位置。
