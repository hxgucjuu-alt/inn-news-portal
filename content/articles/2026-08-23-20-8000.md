  # Africa's Great Green Wall Project Spans 20 Countries, Planting an 8,000-Kilometer Tree Barrier Against Desertification
  
  > Twenty countries in Africa's Sahel region are jointly advancing the "Great Green Wall" project, aiming to plant a tree wall 15 kilometers wide and over 7,700 kilometers long to halt the Sahara Desert's continued southward advance and restore degraded land.
  
  The African continent faces a severe threat from desertification, as the Sahara Desert continues to expand southward, encroaching on farmland and pastureland in the Sahel region. To address this ecological crisis, the African Union has spearheaded the "Great Green Wall" project, linking approximately 20 countries in the Sahel region in an attempt to create a tree-planting barrier across Africa at the southern edge of the Sahara Desert.
  
  According to a report by CitiOrange, the project plans to plant a tree wall approximately 15 kilometers wide and 7,775 kilometers long, spanning the eastern and western sides of the African continent, and is regarded as a major transnational initiative to combat climate change and land degradation.
  
  The core concept of this large-scale ecological project is to establish a green buffer zone between the Sahara Desert and the Sahel grasslands through tree planting and sustainable land management. The Sahel region, located on the southern edge of the Sahara, spans countries including Senegal, Mauritania, Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger, Nigeria, Chad, Sudan, Eritrea, Ethiopia, and Djibouti, and is one of Africa's most fragile ecological transition zones. Local residents have long relied on agriculture and livestock for their livelihoods, but intensifying drought, soil degradation, and vegetation loss have led to unstable food production, forcing millions of people into a livelihood crisis.
  
  The Great Green Wall project is not merely afforestation; it is positioned as an integrated land restoration strategy, aiming to restore ecosystem services and create employment opportunities for local communities.
  
  Although multiple public sources share a common description of the project's overall goals, there are discrepancies in specific data and implementation details. The CitiOrange report states that the tree wall is approximately 7,775 kilometers long and planned to be 15 kilometers wide, emphasizing the participation of 20 countries. However, other international organizations and research institutions have offered different estimates in subsequent tracking of actual planting progress and survival rates. Some reports indicate that, as of recent statistics, the Great Green Wall project's completion rate is only about 15% to 20%, far below the original 2030 target.
  
  Moreover, implementation progress varies greatly between countries; for example, Senegal has planted over 12 million trees, while some Central African countries have fallen significantly behind due to unstable security situations. These data discrepancies reflect the complexity of implementing large-scale transnational ecological projects in practice and serve as a reminder that conclusions should not be drawn from a single source's figures.
  
  The Great Green Wall project has broad impacts on relevant African regions, spanning ecological, agricultural, economic, and social dimensions. Ecologically, tree planting and soil conservation measures help reduce wind and water erosion, increase soil organic matter content, and restore groundwater recharge functions. For Sahelian residents dependent on agriculture, improved land productivity is directly linked to food security and income stability. Some countries have also integrated the Great Green Wall with industries such as renewable energy and ecotourism, attempting to create green job opportunities and slow youth out-migration. However, the project has also sparked controversies over land use rights and resource allocation.
  
  Some scholars and local organizations point out that if traditional land use practices of local communities are not fully respected, large-scale tree planting could crowd out grazing and farming space, potentially even forcing indigenous peoples and herders to relocate. Therefore, the project's success depends not only on the number of trees planted, but also on whether inclusive governance mechanisms are established.
  
  Different perspectives and limitations suggest that external interpretations of the Great Green Wall project should remain cautious. Supporters view it as a model of African countries independently addressing climate change, demonstrating the potential of transnational cooperation; critics, however, note that the project still faces severe challenges in funding, technical support, and long-term maintenance. Some studies further question whether large-scale monoculture planting under extremely arid climatic conditions can achieve the expected benefits, and may even exacerbate ecological stress through competition for water. Additionally, security issues are a major variable; the Sahel region has frequently experienced armed conflict and extremist activity in recent years, rendering some planting areas inaccessible and seriously affecting implementation progress.
  
  Since currently available public data mostly focuses on the project's initial goals and phased results, and lacks a unified monitoring and evaluation mechanism, the reliability of various data points still requires further verification.
  
  Future observation should focus on the Great Green Wall project's funding availability, differences in implementation progress among countries, long-term tracking of tree survival rates and ecological benefits, and the degree of local community participation. Whether the international community continues to provide financial and technical support will be a key factor in the project's success or failure. At the same time, whether the African Union and national governments can maintain momentum amid security challenges and establish transparent performance evaluation indicators deserves close attention. The Great Green Wall is not just an ecological barrier; it also tests African nations' governance capacity and willingness for regional cooperation in the era of climate change, and its developmental experience will provide important references for other regions facing desertification threats.

# 非洲綠色長城計畫跨20國 8000公里植樹對抗沙漠化

> 非洲薩赫爾地區20國聯手推動「綠色長城」計畫，目標種植寬15公里、長逾7700公里的樹牆，抵擋撒哈拉沙漠持續南侵，並恢復退化土地生機。

非洲大陸正面臨嚴峻的沙漠化威脅，撒哈拉沙漠（Sahara）持續向南擴張，侵蝕薩赫爾（Sahel）地帶的耕地與牧場。為應對這場生態危機，非洲聯盟（African Union）主導發起「綠色長城」（Great Green Wall）計畫，串聯薩赫爾地區約20個國家，企圖在撒哈拉沙漠南端打造一道橫跨非洲的植樹屏障。
根據CitiOrange公民報橘的報導，這項計畫準備種植一道寬約15公里、長約7775公里的樹牆，範圍涵蓋非洲大陸東西兩側，被視為對抗氣候變遷與土地退化的重大跨國行動。
這項大型生態工程的核心概念，是透過植樹與永續土地管理，在撒哈拉沙漠與薩赫爾草原之間建立綠色緩衝帶。薩赫爾地帶位於撒哈拉沙漠南緣，橫跨塞內加爾、茅利塔尼亞、馬利、布吉納法索、尼日、奈及利亞、查德、蘇丹、厄利垂亞、衣索比亞、吉布地等國家，是非洲最脆弱的生態過渡區之一。當地居民長期依賴農業與畜牧維生，但乾旱加劇、土壤劣化與植被消失，導致糧食生產不穩定，迫使數百萬人面臨生計危機。
綠色長城計畫不僅是植樹造林，更被定位為整合性的土地復育戰略，期望恢復生態系統服務功能，並為在地社群創造就業機會。
多個公開來源雖然對計畫的整體目標有共同描述，但在具體資料與執行細節上存在差異。CitiOrange公民報橘的報導指出，這道樹牆長度約為7775公里，寬度則規劃為15公里，並強調由20個國家共同參與。然而，其他國際組織與研究機構在後續追蹤中，對實際種植進度與存活率提出不同估計。部分報告顯示，截至近年統計，綠色長城計畫的完成度僅約15%至20%，遠低於最初設定的2030年目標。
此外，不同國家之間的執行進度落差極大，例如塞內加爾已種植超過1200萬棵樹，而部分中非國家則因安全域性勢不穩，計畫推進明顯落後。這些資料差異反映出跨國大型生態計畫在實務推動上的複雜性，也提醒外界不宜以單一來源的數字作為定論。
綠色長城計畫對非洲相關地區的影響層面廣泛，涵蓋生態、農業、經濟與社會等多重面向。在生態方面，植樹與土壤保育措施有助於減少風蝕與水蝕，提高土壤有機質含量，並恢復地下水補注功能。對於仰賴農業的薩赫爾居民而言，土地生產力的提升直接關係到糧食安全與收入穩定。部分國家更將綠色長城與再生能源、生態旅遊等產業結合，試圖創造綠色就業機會，減緩青年人口外流。然而，這項計畫也引發土地利用權與資源分配的爭議。
部分學者與在地組織指出，若未充分尊重當地社群的傳統土地使用方式，大規模植樹可能排擠放牧與農耕空間，甚至導致原住民與牧民被迫遷移。因此，計畫的成功與否，不僅取決於種樹數量，更在於是否建立包容性的治理機制。
不同觀點與限制條件顯示，外界對綠色長城計畫的解讀應保持審慎。支持者認為，這是非洲國家自主應對氣候變遷的典範，展現跨國合作的潛力；但批評者則指出，計畫在資金籌措、技術支援與長期維護方面仍面臨嚴峻挑戰。部分研究更質疑，在極度乾旱的氣候條件下，大規模單一樹種種植可能無法達到預期效益，甚至可能因水分競爭而加劇生態壓力。此外，安全問題也是重大變數，薩赫爾地區近年來頻傳武裝衝突與極端主義活動，導致部分植樹區域無法進入，嚴重影響執行進度。
由於目前可取得的公開資料多集中於計畫初期目標與階段性成果，缺乏統一的監測與評估機制，因此各項資料的可靠性仍有待進一步驗證。
後續觀察重點應放在綠色長城計畫的資金到位情況、各國執行進度差異、樹木存活率與生態效益的長期追蹤，以及在地社群的參與程度。國際社會是否持續提供財務與技術支援，將是影響計畫成敗的關鍵因素。同時，非洲聯盟與各國政府能否在安全挑戰下維持推動動能，並建立透明的成效評估指標，也值得密切關注。綠色長城不僅是一道生態屏障，更考驗非洲國家在氣候變遷時代的治理能力與區域合作意願，其發展經驗將為其他面臨沙漠化威脅的地區提供重要參考。
