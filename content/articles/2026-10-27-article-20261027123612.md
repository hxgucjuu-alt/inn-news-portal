  # Greece Implements Social Media Ban for Under-15s, Drawing Attention to Child Rights Protection in Central America and the Caribbean
  
  > The Greek Parliament has passed legislation banning children under 15 from using social media, with non-compliant platforms facing fines of up to 6% of their global revenue. This move has sparked global discussions on digital protection policies and serves as a cross-border reference for child protection issues in Central America and the Caribbean.
  
  On October 23, 2026, the Greek Parliament officially passed a landmark bill explicitly prohibiting children under the age of 15 from using social media. The Greek Ministry of Digital Governance announced that the law will take effect on January 1, 2027. First proposed by Greek Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis in April of this year, the policy aims to mandate a minimum age threshold of 15 for social media use through legislation, shifting the responsibility of enforcement from parental supervision to social media platform operators. Under the bill, platforms must implement age verification; failure to do so effectively could result in massive fines of up to 6% of their global revenue.
  
  This move represents not only a major shift in European digital governance but also provides a stringent legislative model for countries worldwide facing issues of digital addiction and child online safety.
  
  According to cross-referenced public information, the Greek government adopted a relatively flexible technical approach when promoting this bill. While platform operators are mandated to verify user age, the government does not require the use of a single specific technology, instead allowing operators to use tools such as the Greek government-promoted "Kids Wallet." The core function of this verification program is solely to confirm whether a user meets the age threshold, rather than collecting excessive personal private data.
  
  Currently, the Greek Ministry of Digital Governance has clearly stated that the focus of the bill's enforcement is to ensure platforms take responsibility for oversight, thereby reducing the risk of children being exposed to harmful digital content. The progress of this policy's implementation and its penalty standards have become key indicators for the international community in evaluating the effectiveness of digital platform regulation.
  
  However, there remain several uncertainties regarding the specific implementation details and technical feasibility of the bill. Although the Greek government has proposed "Kids Wallet" as a verification tool, it remains unclear how various social media platforms will integrate such official systems, or whether multinational corporations will face technical delays when complying with localized Greek regulations. Furthermore, there are differing opinions within the legal and technology sectors regarding the calculation basis for the "6% global revenue" fine and how to ensure that adolescent privacy rights are not excessively infringed upon during the enforcement process.
  
  Some experts question whether mandatory age verification will lead to platforms collecting excessive user data, potentially creating new risks of privacy breaches while attempting to protect children. These points of contention remain a focus of attention for all parties before the bill is officially implemented.
  
  The influence of this policy has spread globally, particularly providing significant policy reference value for Central America and the Caribbean, regions that have long faced challenges in child protection. According to reports from UNICEF and relevant international organizations, children in Central America and the Caribbean have long faced multiple threats from climate change, natural disasters, and the digital divide. In these regions, digital platforms are not only channels for information access but often serve as vital tools for children to seek aid or maintain educational contact during disasters.
  
  If the Greek ban model were introduced to these regions, it would face challenges from a vastly different social context. Particularly in situations where digital infrastructure is not yet widespread and educational resources are unevenly distributed, whether a total ban on social media can achieve protective effects—or instead lead to a further deprivation of children's digital rights—is a subject that policymakers must carefully evaluate.
  
  From different perspectives, supporters believe that the Greek legislation is a necessary response to the mental health crisis among children in the digital age and can effectively curb cyberbullying and addictive behaviors. However, opponents worry that this move might drive adolescents toward the dark web or non-mainstream platforms that are harder to regulate, thereby rendering protection mechanisms ineffective. Furthermore, for Central America and the Caribbean, local child protection priorities are largely focused on the right to survival and basic education, and the issue of digital platform regulation is still in its infancy. Therefore, the Greek model should not be over-interpreted as a universal "panacea."
  
  In resource-scarce regions, the positive benefits of digital tools sometimes outweigh the negative impacts. Blindly following a strict prohibition policy could lead to a decline in children's survival competitiveness in the digital age, which is a limitation that the international community must consider when discussing digital policies for children.
  
  Subsequent observation will focus on how the Greek government handles administrative litigation from platform operators after the law takes effect on January 1, 2027, and whether this move will trigger a chain reaction among other EU member states. At the same time, international observers will continue to track whether child rights organizations in Central America and the Caribbean will propose regional strategies regarding digital safety. Balancing digital governance with children's human rights will be a core issue for global public policy in the coming years.
  
  When formulating relevant regulations, governments must not only consider technical feasibility but also tailor inclusive and flexible protective measures to the specificities of their national social structures to ensure that children in the digital age can grow up in a safe and healthy environment.

# 希臘推行15歲以下社群禁令，中美洲與加勒比海地區兒童權益保護備受關注

> 希臘國會透過法案，禁止15歲以下兒童使用社群媒體，違規平臺面臨最高6%全球營收罰款。此舉引發全球對於數位保護政策的討論，亦對中美洲及加勒比海地區的兒童保護議題產生跨國借鏡效應。


希臘國會於2026年10月23日正式透過一項具指標性的法案，明文禁止15歲以下兒童使用社群媒體，並由希臘數位治理部宣佈，該法將於2027年1月1日正式生效。此項政策最早由希臘總理Kyriakos Mitsotakis於今年4月提出，旨在透過立法手段，將社群媒體使用的年齡門檻強制設定為15歲，並將執行責任從家長監督轉移至社群平臺業者。根據法案規定，平臺必須落實使用者年齡驗證，若未能有效執行，最高將面臨全球營收6%的鉅額罰款。
此舉不僅是歐洲數位治理的重大變革，也為全球各國在面對數位成癮與兒童網路安全議題時，提供了一種強硬的立法範本。
根據多方公開資訊交叉比對，希臘政府在推動此項法案時，採取了相對彈性的技術執行路徑。平臺業者雖被強制要求驗證使用者年齡，但政府並不強制要求採用單一技術，而是開放業者使用如希臘官方推動的「Kids Wallet」等工具進行驗證。該驗證程式的核心僅在於確認使用者是否符合年齡門檻，而非蒐集過多個人隱私資料。
目前，希臘數位治理部已明確表示，法案的執行重心在於確保平臺負起審查責任，藉此降低兒童接觸有害數位內容的風險，這項政策的落實進度與罰則標準，已成為國際間評估數位平臺監管成效的重要指標。
然而，關於該法案的具體執行細節與技術可行性，目前仍存在若干尚待確認之處。儘管希臘政府提出「Kids Wallet」作為驗證工具，但各類社群平臺如何整合此類官方系統，以及跨國企業在面對希臘在地化法規時，是否會產生技術對接上的延遲，目前尚無定論。此外，針對「全球營收6%」的罰款計算基準，以及在執行過程中如何保障青少年隱私權不被過度侵害，法律界與科技界仍有不同聲音。
部分專家質疑，強制性的年齡驗證是否會導致平臺過度蒐集使用者資料，反而在保護兒童的同時，創造了新的隱私外洩風險，這些爭議點在法案正式實施前，仍是各界關注的焦點。
此項政策的影響力已擴散至全球，特別是對於長期面臨兒童保護挑戰的中美洲與加勒比海地區，產生了顯著的政策參考價值。根據聯合國兒童基金會（UNICEF）與相關國際組織的報告，中美洲與加勒比海地區的兒童長期面臨氣候變遷、自然災害以及數位落差的多重威脅。在這些地區，數位平臺不僅是資訊獲取的管道，也常成為兒童在災害中尋求援助或維持教育聯絡的重要工具。
希臘的禁令模式若被引入這些地區，將面臨截然不同的社會脈絡挑戰，特別是在數位基礎建設尚未普及、教育資源分配不均的情況下，全面禁止社群媒體是否能達到保護效果，抑或會造成兒童數位權益的進一步剝奪，成為政策制定者必須審慎評估的課題。
從不同觀點來看，支持者認為希臘的立法是回應數位時代兒童心理健康危機的必要之舉，能有效遏止網路霸凌與成癮行為；然而，反對者則擔憂此舉可能導致青少年轉向更難監管的暗網或非主流平臺，反而讓保護機制失效。此外，對於中美洲與加勒比海地區而言，當地的兒童保護重點多集中於生存權與基本教育權，數位平臺的監管議題尚處於萌芽階段。因此，不應過度解讀希臘模式為全球通用的「萬靈丹」。
在資源匱乏的地區，數位工具的正面效益有時大於負面衝擊，盲目跟進強硬的禁令政策，可能導致兒童在數位時代的生存競爭力下降，這也是國際社會在討論兒童數位政策時，必須考量的侷限性。
後續觀察重點將集中在2027年1月1日法案實施後，希臘政府如何處理平臺業者的行政訴訟，以及此舉是否會引發歐盟其他成員國的連鎖效應。同時，國際觀察家亦將持續追蹤中美洲與加勒比海地區的兒童權益組織，是否會針對數位安全議題提出區域性的因應策略。在數位治理與兒童人權之間如何取得平衡，將是未來數年全球公共政策的核心議題。
各國政府在制定相關法規時，不僅需考量技術層面的可行性，更需針對各國社會結構的特殊性，制定具備包容性與彈性的保護措施，以確保數位時代的兒童能夠在安全且健康的環境中成長。
