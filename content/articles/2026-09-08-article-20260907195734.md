  # Security Challenges in Central America and the Caribbean: A Dual Test of Transnational Crime and Climate Diplomacy
  
  > Central America and the Caribbean are currently facing severe security threats and challenges in climate diplomacy. Panamanian authorities have confirmed that organized crime is rampant, while countries in the region are actively seeking resilience through climate diplomacy to address global shifts.
  
  Central America and the Caribbean have long been critical zones for global geopolitics and economic development. The region not only faces complex issues of transnational crime but is also attempting to seek regional resilience through diplomatic means under the impact of climate change. According to a report by *Prensa Latina*, Panamanian government officials recently publicly acknowledged that organized crime poses a significant threat to the country's social stability.
  
  Meanwhile, the think tank "Inter-American Dialogue" launched a roundtable series titled "New Trends in Climate Diplomacy" on May 21, 2026, aimed at exploring policy comparisons and cooperation models for the Caribbean and Pacific regions within a changing global framework. These events highlight the urgent needs of the region in the two major areas of security governance and environmental sustainability.
  
  According to comprehensive reports from *Prensa Latina* and *News in America*, Panamanian Minister of Government Dinoska Montalvo and National Police Chief Jaime Fernandez have clearly indicated that the influence of organized crime in the country is continuing to expand. This phenomenon is not an isolated incident in a single country, but a microcosm of the deteriorating security landscape across Central America.
  
  At the same time, academic and policy research institutions such as "The Caribbean Council" and "Inter-American Dialogue" continue to track the policy direction of the region, jointly confirming that climate resilience and regional cooperation have become the top diplomatic agenda for Caribbean governments. Especially when facing extreme climate and economic transformation pressures, countries are seeking closer partnerships to maintain regional stability.
  
  Although attention to regional security and climate challenges has increased significantly, there are still different perspectives and information gaps regarding specific response strategies. For example, *Prensa Latina* emphasizes the sense of crisis in security enforcement, focusing on government officials' acknowledgment of the current state of crime and their determination to combat it; however, academia, such as the "Inter-American Dialogue," tends to analyze from the perspective of long-term foreign policy and climate resilience, focusing the issue on the establishment of international cooperation frameworks.
  
  Furthermore, regarding the specific operational models and capital flows of criminal organizations, as well as the details of specific cooperation consensuses reached by various countries at the climate diplomacy roundtable, there is currently a lack of unified official data or public archives. The differences in the focus of media reports reflect the complexity of regional issues in terms of security and development.
  
  These regional issues have had a profound impact on the industries and society of Central America and the Caribbean. In terms of security, rampant criminal activity directly impacts the local investment environment and the development of the tourism industry. Although, as mentioned by *Anywhere*, winter tourism in places like Belize remains attractive, security issues are undoubtedly the biggest concern for the continued growth of the regional tourism industry. At the policy level, the advancement of climate diplomacy is crucial for Caribbean island nations that rely on agriculture and marine resources. If infrastructure resilience cannot be improved through transnational cooperation, the region will face the risk of food insecurity and economic collapse.
  
  Therefore, the success or failure of security governance and climate policy will directly determine the level of social stability and economic prosperity in the region over the next decade.
  
  Regarding the aforementioned challenges, the outside world should not over-interpret the effectiveness of a single policy or simplify regional problems into the result of a single factor. First, the Panamanian government's acknowledgment of organized crime shows the immense pressure faced by the law enforcement system, but the solution lies not only in strengthening police forces, but also in long-term projects involving social welfare and judicial reform. Second, although climate diplomacy is an opportunity for regional cooperation, differences in resource allocation, economic development levels, and political systems among countries make the implementation of any transnational agreement challenging. Analysts remind that the autonomy of Central American countries in the face of external pressure should not be ignored; over-reliance on external aid or the frameworks of international organizations may not solve deep-seated structural contradictions.
  
  Subsequent observation should focus on the specific law enforcement effectiveness of Panama and surrounding countries in combating transnational crime, and whether the "New Trends in Climate Diplomacy" roundtable can be translated into specific transnational action plans. In addition, as global climate change intensifies, whether climate resilience cooperation in the Caribbean and Pacific regions can be expanded to broader economic and trade areas will be a key indicator for assessing the region's future development resilience. The international community and regional organizations should continue to pay attention to the implementation progress of these policies and observe how governments balance domestic public opinion and international diplomatic obligations when facing security threats and environmental challenges; this will be the core variable determining the future stability of the Central American and Caribbean region.

# 中美洲與加勒比海地區安全挑戰：跨國犯罪與氣候外交的雙重考驗

> 中美洲與加勒比海地區近期面臨嚴峻的安全威脅與氣候外交挑戰。巴拿馬當局證實有組織犯罪活動猖獗，同時區域內各國正積極透過氣候外交尋求韌性合作，以應對全球局勢變遷。


中美洲與加勒比海地區長期以來是全球地緣政治與經濟發展的關鍵地帶，該區域不僅面臨複雜的跨國犯罪問題，亦在氣候變遷的衝擊下，試圖透過外交手段尋求區域韌性。根據《Prensa Latina》報導，巴拿馬政府官員近期公開承認，有組織犯罪對該國社會穩定構成重大威脅。
與此同時，智庫「美洲對話」（Inter-American Dialogue）於2026年5月21日啟動了名為「氣候外交新潮流」的圓桌會議系列，旨在探討加勒比海與太平洋地區在變動全球架構下的政策比較與合作模式。這些事件凸顯了該地區在安全治理與環境永續兩大領域的迫切需求。
根據《Prensa Latina》與《News in America》的綜合報導，巴拿馬政府部長迪諾斯卡·蒙塔爾沃（Dinoska Montalvo）與國家警察局長海梅·費爾南德斯（Jaime Fernandez）已明確指出，有組織犯罪在該國的影響力正持續擴大。這一現象並非單一國家的孤立事件，而是中美洲整體安全格局惡化的縮影。
與此同時，學術與政策研究機構如「加勒比海委員會」（The Caribbean Council）與「美洲對話」持續追蹤該地區的政策走向，共同確認了氣候韌性與區域合作已成為加勒比海各國政府在外交層面的首要議程，特別是在面對極端氣候與經濟轉型壓力時，各國正尋求更緊密的夥伴關係以維護區域穩定。
儘管各界對於區域安全與氣候挑戰的關注度顯著提升，但在具體因應策略上仍存在不同觀點與資訊落差。例如，《Prensa Latina》強調的是安全執法層面的危機感，側重於政府官員對於犯罪現狀的承認與打擊決心；然而，學術界如「美洲對話」則傾向於從長期的外交政策與氣候韌性角度剖析，將議題聚焦於國際合作架構的建立。
此外，關於犯罪組織的具體運作模式、資金流向，以及氣候外交圓桌會議中各國達成的具體合作共識細節，目前尚缺乏統一的官方資料或公開檔案說明，各媒體報導側重點的差異，反映出該地區議題在安全與發展層面上的複雜性。
這些區域性議題對中美洲與加勒比海的產業與社會產生了深遠影響。在安全方面，猖獗的犯罪活動直接衝擊了當地投資環境與旅遊業的發展，儘管如《Anywhere》所提及，貝里斯等地的冬季旅遊仍具吸引力，但治安問題無疑是區域旅遊業持續成長的最大隱憂。在政策層面，氣候外交的推進對於依賴農業與海洋資源的加勒比海島國至關重要，若無法透過跨國合作提升基礎設施的韌性，該地區將面臨糧食安全與經濟崩潰的風險。
因此，安全治理與氣候政策的成敗，將直接決定該地區未來十年的社會穩定與經濟繁榮程度。
針對上述挑戰，外界不應過度解讀單一政策的成效，或將區域問題簡化為單一因素的結果。首先，巴拿馬政府對有組織犯罪的承認，顯示了執法體系面臨的巨大壓力，但解決之道不僅在於警力強化，更涉及社會福利與司法改革的長期工程。其次，氣候外交雖是區域合作的契機，但各國在資源分配、經濟發展水準與政治體制上的差異，使得任何跨國協議的落實都面臨挑戰。分析人士提醒，不應忽視中美洲各國在面對外部壓力時的自主性，過度依賴外部援助或國際組織的框架，可能無法解決深層的結構性矛盾。
後續觀察重點應放在巴拿馬及周邊國家在打擊跨國犯罪的具體執法成效，以及「氣候外交新潮流」圓桌會議後續是否能轉化為具體的跨國行動計畫。此外，隨著全球氣候變遷加劇，加勒比海與太平洋地區的氣候韌性合作是否能擴大至更廣泛的經濟貿易領域，將是評估該區域未來發展韌性的關鍵指標。國際社會與區域組織應持續關注這些政策的落實進度，並觀察各國政府在面對安全威脅與環境挑戰時，如何平衡國內民意與國際外交義務，這將是決定中美洲與加勒比海地區未來穩定與否的核心變數。
