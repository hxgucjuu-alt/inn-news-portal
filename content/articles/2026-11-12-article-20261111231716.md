  # Climate Change Reshapes the Global Public Health Landscape: Infectious Disease Surveillance and Pandemic Challenges in South America
  
  > Experts at the Sustainability Expo in Bangkok have issued a warning that climate change is accelerating the expansion of infectious disease transmission. Regions such as South America face the risk of re-exposure to pathogens, necessitating an urgent strengthening of transnational surveillance and response mechanisms within public health systems.
  
  The impact of climate change on global public health has become a focal point of international concern. On November 7, 2026, during the Sustainability Expo held at the Queen Sirikit National Convention Center in Bangkok, Thailand, numerous medical and environmental experts conducted an in-depth discussion on climate change and infectious disease risks. Attendees pointed out that rising global temperatures not only lead to frequent extreme weather events but also significantly alter the transmission pathways of vectors and viruses, causing diseases previously confined to specific geographical environments to spread toward higher latitudes or higher altitudes.
  
  For South America, the ecosystems of the Andes Mountains and the Amazon rainforest are fragile. The drastic environmental changes brought about by climate change are highly likely to lead to the resurgence of existing local infectious diseases and even increase the probability of cross-species transmission of emerging pathogens, posing a severe challenge to public health security within the region.
  
  According to the consensus reached by various experts at the conference, climate change is the core driver behind the shifting geographical distribution of infectious diseases. Yong Poovorawan, a virology expert at the Faculty of Medicine at Chulalongkorn University, and Alisa Yanasarn, a physician at the World Health Organization (WHO) representative office in Thailand, agreed that permafrost melting and habitat destruction caused by global warming are releasing ancient pathogens that have long lain dormant in the natural environment.
  
  This phenomenon is particularly sensitive in the tropical rainforests and mountainous regions of South America, as vegetation changes and animal migration caused by climate anomalies directly shorten the distance between humans and wildlife, thereby increasing the risk of zoonotic disease outbreaks. Experts at the meeting emphasized that current public health systems must shift from "reactive epidemic prevention" to "preventive surveillance" and establish a global early warning system.
  
  However, there remain differences in perspectives regarding the specific risk assessment of pathogen re-exposure. Environmental and health expert Sonthi Koc pointed out that while the global warming trend is clear, the probability of pathogen resurgence in different geographical regions is still limited by variables such as soil composition, altitude, and local biodiversity; it is currently impossible to assert which specific pathogens will trigger outbreaks in South America first. Furthermore, participants expressed reservations regarding whether existing public health systems possess sufficient laboratory diagnostic capabilities to identify these "ancient pathogens."
  
  Some experts believe that current gene sequencing and tracking technologies for pathogens in extreme environments are still in their infancy, and transnational data-sharing mechanisms have not yet been fully established, which has become the greatest unknown and technical bottleneck in the current epidemic prevention system.
  
  In terms of specific impacts, the infectious disease risks triggered by climate change have already had a profound effect on South America's socio-economy and public policy. First, in terms of policy formulation, South American governments are facing immense fiscal pressure and must strike a balance between responding to climate disasters and enhancing medical resilience. Second, for industries, agriculture and tourism—the pillars of the South American economy—are highly susceptible to the negative impacts of disease outbreaks; if the transmission range of vector-borne diseases expands, it will directly impact labor health and international travel confidence.
  
  Furthermore, the allocation of public health resources also faces challenges. How to establish effective medical surveillance stations in remote Andean regions while maintaining epidemic prevention capacity in urban areas has become a top priority for regional health policy.
  
  Although the risks brought by climate change are evident, experts cautioned against over-interpreting the threat of pathogen resurgence to avoid inciting social panic. The core of public health lies in strengthening infrastructure and improving public health education, rather than simply making fear-based predictions about specific pathogens. A limiting factor is that there is still a data gap in global cross-disciplinary research on climate change and microbiology, and many model predictions still lack verification from long-term field sampling. Therefore, viewing climate change as a singular "disease maker" is an oversimplification. In fact, human activity, land-use changes, and disparities in basic medical conditions are often the key variables that determine whether an infectious disease evolves into a pandemic.
  
  Subsequent observation should focus on the progress of cooperation between international organizations and regional health agencies in South America (such as the Pan American Health Organization). First, whether countries can implement real-time sharing of transnational pathogen surveillance data will be an indicator of the effectiveness of epidemic prevention mechanisms. Second, whether vaccine development and antiviral drug stockpiles for climate-sensitive areas can receive sufficient international financial support is also a key point to watch. Finally, as global climate conferences continue, whether countries can formally incorporate public health security into climate change mitigation and adaptation policies will determine the success or failure of the global fight against emerging infectious diseases over the next decade.
  
  Experts suggest that countries should continue to strengthen primary healthcare surveillance and invest in basic research on environmental virology to cope with the unpredictability brought about by climate change.

# 氣候變遷重塑全球公衛版圖：南美洲傳染病監測與防疫挑戰

> 專家於曼谷永續發展博覽會示警，氣候變遷正加速傳染病傳播範圍擴大，南美洲等地區面臨病原體重新暴露風險，公共衛生體系亟需強化跨國監測與應變機制。


氣候變遷對全球公共衛生的衝擊已成為國際社會關注焦點。2026年11月7日，於泰國曼谷詩麗吉王后國家會議中心舉行的永續發展博覽會中，多位醫學與環境專家針對氣候變遷與傳染病風險進行深入探討。與會者指出，全球氣溫升高不僅導致極端天氣頻發，更顯著改變了病媒蚊與病毒的傳播路徑，使得原本受限於特定地理環境的疾病，開始向高緯度或高海拔地區擴散。
對於南美洲而言，安地斯山脈與亞馬遜雨林區域的生態系統脆弱，氣候變遷帶來的環境劇變，極可能導致當地既有傳染病再度流行，甚至增加新興病原體跨物種傳播的機率，對區域內的公共衛生安全構成嚴峻挑戰。
根據多方專家在會議中的共同確認，氣候變遷是導致傳染病地理分佈改變的核心驅動力。朱拉隆功大學醫學院病毒學專家雍（Yong Poovorawan）與世界衛生組織（WHO）駐泰國代表處醫師阿莉莎（Alisa Yanasarn）一致認為，全球暖化導致的永久凍土融化與生態棲地破壞，正將長期沉睡於自然環境中的古老病原體重新釋放。
這種現象在南美洲的熱帶雨林與高山地區尤為敏感，因為氣候異常造成的植被與動物遷徙，會直接縮短人類與野生動物的接觸距離，進而提高人畜共通傳染病的爆發風險。與會專家強調，當前各國公衛體系必須從「反應式防疫」轉向「預防式監測」，建立全球性的早期預警系統。
然而，針對病原體重新暴露的具體風險評估，各界仍存在觀點上的差異。環境與健康專家頌提（Sonthi Koc）指出，雖然全球氣候暖化趨勢明確，但不同地理區域的病原體復甦機率仍受限於土壤成分、海拔高度及當地生物多樣性等變數，目前尚無法斷言哪些特定病原體將在南美洲優先引發流行。此外，針對現有公衛體系是否具備足夠的實驗室診斷能力以識別這些「古老病原體」，與會者之間存在保留態度。
部分專家認為，目前對於極端環境下病原體的基因定序與追蹤技術仍處於起步階段，跨國資料共享機制尚未完全建立，這成為當前防疫體系中最大的未知數與技術瓶頸。
在具體影響層面，氣候變遷引發的傳染病風險已對南美洲的社會經濟與公共政策產生深遠衝擊。首先，在政策制定上，南美各國政府正面臨巨大的財政壓力，必須在應對氣候災害與提升醫療韌性之間取得平衡。其次，對於產業而言，農業與觀光業作為南美洲的經濟支柱，極易受到傳染病爆發的負面影響；若病媒蚊傳播範圍擴大，將直接衝擊勞動力健康與國際旅遊信心。
此外，公共衛生資源的分配也面臨挑戰，如何在偏遠的安地斯山區建立有效的醫療監測站，同時維持城市地區的防疫能量，已成為區域衛生政策的當務之急。
儘管氣候變遷帶來的風險顯而易見，但專家提醒，不應過度解讀病原體復甦的威脅而引發社會恐慌。公共衛生的核心在於強化基礎設施與提升民眾的衛生教育，而非單純針對特定病原體進行恐懼式預測。限制因素在於，目前全球對於氣候變遷與微生物學的交叉研究仍存在資料斷層，許多模型預測仍缺乏長期現場取樣的驗證。因此，將氣候變遷視為單一的「疾病製造者」過於簡化，事實上，人類活動、土地利用變更以及基礎醫療條件的落差，往往才是決定傳染病是否演變成大流行的關鍵變數。
後續觀察重點應聚焦於國際組織與南美區域性衛生機構（如泛美衛生組織）的合作進展。首先，各國是否能落實跨國病原體監測資料的即時共享，將是檢驗防疫機制有效性的指標。其次，針對氣候變遷敏感地區的疫苗研發與抗病毒藥物儲備，是否能獲得足夠的國際資金支援，亦是觀察重點。最後，隨著全球氣候會議的持續推進，各國能否將公共衛生安全正式納入氣候變遷的減緩與調適政策中，將決定未來十年全球對抗新興傳染病的成敗。
專家建議，各國應持續強化基層醫療監測，並投資於環境病毒學的基礎研究，以應對氣候變遷帶來的不可預測性。
