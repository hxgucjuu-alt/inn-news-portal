  # Global AI Governance and Infrastructure Disputes: Fei-Fei Li Calls for Third-Party Oversight Amidst Data Center Protests
  
  > Stanford professor Fei-Fei Li argues that AI safety assessments should move away from corporate self-regulation toward independent institutional oversight; meanwhile, protests triggered by the expansion of data centers are intensifying globally, highlighting the friction between technological development and community interests.
  
  The rapid evolution of artificial intelligence (AI) technology has not only accelerated the pace of global digital transformation but has also sparked widespread discussion regarding safety governance and infrastructure construction. Fei-Fei Li, a Stanford University professor, AI pioneer, and co-founder of World Labs, recently issued a public warning in an interview with Bloomberg Television, emphasizing that safety assessments for cutting-edge AI systems cannot rely solely on the self-discipline of developers. She stressed the necessity of introducing external evaluations from independent institutions, academia, and the public sector to ensure that technological development does not spiral out of control.
  
  Meanwhile, as demand for AI computing surges, protests against data center construction have emerged in various parts of the world. From Scotland and London to India and Australia, public concern regarding energy consumption, land use, and the impact on local communities is intensifying. Against this backdrop, tech giants and international policymakers are attempting to mitigate the social impact of technological expansion through voluntary standards and localized solutions.
  
  According to public reports, safety governance in the AI industry has reached a critical turning point. Last Tuesday, U.S. President Trump held a meeting with AI industry leaders, including Elon Musk, Mark Zuckerberg, and Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, to reach a consensus on voluntary standards for AI safety. During the meeting, Trump emphasized that technology companies have a responsibility to ensure that data center construction leaves surrounding communities feeling "satisfied."
  
  Fei-Fei Li offered a supplement to this perspective, stating bluntly that the "player-referee" model of self-assessment carries structural risks. She argued that only by establishing credible external evaluation benchmarks can humanity enjoy the dividends of technology while maintaining a safety baseline. This argument echoes long-standing concerns in academia regarding AI "black box" operations and risk management.
  
  Although there is a consensus that "AI needs governance," significant differences and uncertainties remain regarding implementation. The "independent institutional intervention" advocated by Fei-Fei Li currently lacks a concrete legal framework and international enforcement standards. There is no consensus on how to define "independence" or whether evaluation results should be legally binding. Furthermore, there is a clear policy tension between the "voluntary standards" emphasized by the Trump administration and the "external mandatory regulation" called for by Fei-Fei Li.
  
  At the same time, media coverage of protests against data center construction varies by region: social enterprises in Australia tend to promote renewable energy solutions, such as solar power, to appease public grievances. However, other regions, such as India or Scotland, face more complex disputes over land acquisition and power distribution, and there is no unified path to resolution for these local challenges.
  
  The surge in demand for data centers has a direct impact on infrastructure in Southeast Asia and globally. As the hardware backbone of AI computing, data centers not only consume vast amounts of electricity but also place pressure on local water resources and the environment. Taking the Mekong River region as an example, while current focus is largely on water resources and dam level management, the expansion of digital infrastructure into Southeast Asia will inevitably intensify competition for regional power supplies and water resources in the future. Failure to strike a balance between AI construction and local environmental carrying capacity could lead to regional social instability and further hinder the long-term deployment of the AI industry in developing regions.
  
  Current perspectives on AI governance remain limited, and one should avoid over-interpreting a single meeting or the opinion of a single expert. Whether the Trump administration's "voluntary standards" can effectively constrain corporate behavior remains to be seen; meanwhile, the external oversight advocated by Fei-Fei Li will inevitably face significant implementation resistance regarding the protection of trade secrets and the speed of technological innovation. Furthermore, the wave of data center protests reflects a widespread public distrust of "tech giants overriding community interests," and technical solutions alone (such as solar power) may not be sufficient to address deeper issues of social distributive justice. When interpreting such news, the public should be mindful of the gap between technology policy and local public opinion, avoiding the simplification of AI development into a mere technical deployment issue.
  
  Key areas for future observation include whether the U.S. government will transform current "voluntary standards" into a legally binding regulatory framework, and whether the independent evaluation institutions advocated by Fei-Fei Li can garner broad support from international academia and the public sector. In Southeast Asia and other emerging markets, the focus will be on how multinational tech companies respond to environmental protests from local communities, and whether governments will formulate stricter environmental and energy usage regulations for data centers. As AI technology enters the deep-water phase of physical infrastructure construction, balancing "computing power expansion" with "social resilience" will become a core challenge for global technology governance over the next two years.

# 全球AI治理與基礎設施爭議：李飛飛籲第三方監管與資料中心抗議潮

> 史丹佛教授李飛飛主張AI安全評估應脫離企業自評，轉向獨立機構監管；與此同時，全球各地因資料中心擴張引發的抗議聲浪持續升溫，凸顯科技發展與社群利益的摩擦。


人工智慧（AI）技術的飛速演進，不僅推動了全球數位轉型的程式，也同步引發了關於安全治理與基礎設施建設的廣泛討論。史丹佛大學教授、AI先驅兼World Labs共同創辦人李飛飛，近期於彭博電視專訪中公開示警，強調尖端AI系統的安全評估不能僅依賴開發商的自律，必須引入獨立機構、學術界與公共部門的外部評測，以確保技術發展不致失控。
與此同時，隨著AI運算需求激增，全球多地出現針對資料中心建設的抗議活動，從蘇格蘭、倫敦到印度與澳洲，民眾對於能源消耗、土地使用及社群環境影響的擔憂日益加劇。此一背景下，科技巨頭與國際政策制定者正試圖透過自願性標準與在地化解決方案，緩解技術擴張帶來的社會衝擊。
根據公開報導，AI產業的安全治理已進入關鍵轉折點。上週二，美國總統川普與馬斯克（Elon Musk）、祖克柏（Mark Zuckerberg）及Anthropic執行長達裡奧·阿莫代（Dario Amodei）等AI企業高層舉行會談，針對AI安全自願標準達成共識。川普在會中強調，科技公司有責任確保資料中心建設能讓周邊社群感到「滿意」。
李飛飛對此觀點提出補充，她直言「球員兼裁判」的自評模式存在結構性隱憂，唯有建立具公信力的外部評測基準，人類才能在享受技術紅利的同時，守住安全底線。這一論點呼應了學術界對於AI黑箱作業與風險控管的長期憂慮。
儘管各界在「AI需要治理」的大方向上達成共識，但在具體執行層面仍存在顯著差異與未知數。李飛飛主張的「獨立機構介入」，目前仍缺乏具體的法律架構與國際執行標準，對於如何界定「獨立」以及評測結果是否具備強制力，各方說法不一。此外，川普政府強調的「自願標準」與李飛飛所呼籲的「外部強制監管」之間，存在明顯的政策張力。
同時，針對資料中心建設的抗議，各國媒體報導的焦點亦有所不同：澳洲的社會企業傾向於推動太陽能等再生能源解決方案來平息民怨，但其他地區如印度或蘇格蘭，則面臨更為複雜的土地徵收與供電分配爭議，這些地方性挑戰尚未有統一的解決路徑。
資料中心的需求激增對東南亞及全球基礎設施帶來直接影響。作為AI運算的硬體支撐，資料中心不僅消耗龐大電力，亦對在地水資源與環境造成壓力。以湄公河區域為例，雖然當前焦點多集中於水資源與大壩水位管理，但隨著數位基礎設施向東南亞擴張，未來資料中心對區域電力供應與水資源的競爭勢必加劇。若無法在AI建設與地方環境承載力之間取得平衡，恐將導致區域性的社會不穩定，並進一步阻礙AI產業在開發中地區的長期佈局。
目前各界對於AI治理的觀點仍存在限制，不應過度解讀單一會議或單一專家的意見。川普政府的「自願標準」是否能有效約束企業行為，仍有待觀察；而李飛飛所提倡的外部監管，在商業機密保護與技術創新速度之間，勢必面臨巨大的執行阻力。此外，資料中心抗議潮反映的是民眾對「科技巨頭凌駕社群利益」的普遍不信任，單靠技術性解決方案（如太陽能）可能不足以解決深層的社會分配正義問題。公眾在解讀此類新聞時，應留意科技政策與地方民意之間的落差，避免將AI發展簡化為單一的技術部署問題。
後續觀察重點在於，美國政府是否會將目前的「自願標準」轉化為具備法律效力的監管框架，以及李飛飛所提倡的獨立評測機構是否能獲得國際學術與公共部門的廣泛響應。在東南亞與其他新興市場，觀察重點將聚焦於跨國科技公司如何回應在地社群的環保抗議，以及各國政府是否會針對資料中心制定更嚴格的環境與能源使用規範。隨著AI技術進入實體基礎設施建設的深水區，如何平衡「算力擴張」與「社會韌性」，將成為未來兩年全球科技治理的核心課題。
